Another Forgotten Genocide

1933

A revolution that promised bread ended in one of the deadliest famines in modern history. Millions of Ukrainians starved, a Pulitzer winner told the world it wasn’t happening, and a poisonous myth grew up in the space where the truth should have been.

I Peace, Land, Bread

In the fall of 1917, Lenin’s Bolsheviks seized power in Petrograd on three words: peace, land, bread. It is worth remembering how fast the promise turned. Within weeks the new government had a secret police, the Cheka. Within a year, after an assassin’s bullets struck Lenin and another assassin killed the Petrograd Cheka chief, Moisei Uritsky, the regime declared a Red Terror and began shooting hostages by the thousands.

Then came the war on the countryside. Starting in 1929, Stalin forced Soviet peasants off their own land and into collective farms. Families labeled “kulaks,” meaning anyone a little better off or a little too stubborn, were stripped of everything and shipped east in cattle cars. The peasants who were left were told to grow grain for the state. Nowhere did the state want more of it than in Ukraine, the breadbasket of the empire.

II The Harvest

By 1932 the quotas were impossible, and Moscow treated every shortfall as sabotage. A law that August made taking grain from a collective field, even a handful of stalks, punishable by death or ten years in a camp. That fall Stalin sent his top men with emergency powers to squeeze Ukraine and the Kuban region next door, where many Ukrainians lived. Brigades went house to house, probing floors and walls with iron rods for hidden food. Villages that missed their targets were blacklisted and cut off from goods. In January 1933, Stalin and his premier, Vyacheslav Molotov, signed an order sealing the borders so starving peasants could not leave Ukraine to look for food.

People ate bark, grass and pets. Then they died, in their homes and on the roads, in numbers no one counted honestly at the time. The common estimate today is roughly 3.5 to 5 million dead in Ukraine in 1932 and 1933; some historians, including Robert Conquest, put it higher. Ukrainians call it the Holodomor, “death by hunger.”

Here is the detail that should end any argument about whether it was a natural disaster: while Ukraine starved, the Soviet Union kept selling grain abroad. Britannica counts more than a million tons exported to the West during the famine, while Moscow refused outside relief. Food was leaving the country while the people who grew it starved.

III The Men Who Ran It

You will hear it said, in comment sections and on podcasts, that the revolution and its killing were run by Jews. It deserves a straight answer, because part of it is true and the rest is a lie built on top.

The true part: several of the best-known early Bolsheviks were born Jewish. Leon Trotsky, born Lev Bronstein, built the Red Army. Grigory Zinoviev ran the Communist International. Lev Kamenev and Yakov Sverdlov chaired the new government’s executive committee. Uritsky ran the Cheka in Petrograd. Historian Yuri Slezkine, in The Jewish Century, found that in 1935 about 38 percent of the secret police’s leading officers were Jewish, in a country where Jews were under 2 percent of the population. Genrikh Yagoda, born Yenokh Yehuda, ran the secret police and oversaw the Gulag’s explosive growth in the early 1930s. And Lazar Kaganovich, a Jewish-born Politburo member, was one of the emissaries Stalin sent to enforce the grain seizures in Ukraine and the Kuban. In 2010 a court in Kyiv found him guilty of genocide over the Holodomor.

Those are facts, and no honest history should hide them. But they are not the whole story, and the parts left out are what turn history into a conspiracy theory.

Start with who was actually in charge. The man who ordered the famine was Stalin, a Georgian. The order sealing Ukraine’s borders was signed by Stalin and Molotov, a Russian. The same 2010 Kyiv court named seven men responsible; five of them were Georgian, Russian, Polish and Ukrainian. The secret police was founded by Felix Dzerzhinsky, a Pole, and its worst years of killing were run by Nikolai Yezhov and then Lavrentiy Beria, neither of them Jewish. The man who shot Uritsky, for that matter, was a Jewish student named Leonid Kannegisser.

IV The Myth and Its Victims

The Bolsheviks born Jewish had walked away from Judaism. They were atheist revolutionaries, and the state they built went after Jewish life the way it went after every faith. A special Jewish section of the party closed synagogues and religious schools, banned the teaching of Hebrew as “reactionary” and broke up the Zionist movement with the help of the Cheka. Most Russian Jews never joined the Bolsheviks at all; many backed rival socialist parties or wanted nothing to do with any of it.

And then Stalin devoured them. Zinoviev and Kamenev were shot in 1936 after a show trial. Yagoda was shot in 1938. Trotsky was murdered with an ice axe in Mexico in 1940 on Stalin’s orders. The leaders of the party’s Jewish section were killed in the purges. By the end of Stalin’s life, the regime was hunting “rootless cosmopolitans” and inventing a plot by Jewish doctors to poison the leadership.

None of that stopped the lie. The idea that Bolshevism was a Jewish plot, what historians call the myth of “Judeo-Bolshevism,” spread across Europe within months of the revolution. It fed pogroms during the Russian Civil War that killed, by many counts, around 100,000 Jews. Hitler made it a pillar of Nazi propaganda. When Germany invaded the Soviet Union in 1941, the war was sold as a crusade against “Jewish Bolshevism,” and the SS death squads that followed the army shot Jewish families by the hundreds of thousands under that banner. Yad Vashem, Israel’s Holocaust memorial, counts the myth among the ideas that laid the groundwork for the Holocaust.

So the honest version runs like this. Some of the revolution’s leaders were born Jewish. The regime was run by men of many backgrounds, answered to a Georgian dictator, crushed Judaism, and murdered most of those same Jewish-born leaders. And the claim that Jews were behind it all became a justification for murdering millions of Jews who had nothing to do with it.

V Why We Forgot

In March 1933 a 27-year-old Welsh reporter named Gareth Jones slipped into the Ukrainian countryside, walked from village to village, and came out to tell a press conference in Berlin what he had seen: people dying of hunger everywhere. Two days later the most famous American correspondent in Moscow, Walter Duranty of The New York Times, answered him in print under the headline “Russians Hungry, But Not Starving.” Duranty had won a Pulitzer Prize the year before for his Soviet coverage. His version became the accepted one. Jones was banned from the Soviet Union and was killed two years later while reporting in Inner Mongolia. In 2003 the Pulitzer board reviewed Duranty’s prize and declined to take it back.

Moscow, for its part, denied the famine for as long as the Soviet Union existed, and its archives stayed closed until 1991. Only in the last two decades has recognition caught up: about 35 countries now formally call the famine a genocide. The United Nations has never made a binding ruling, and Russia still insists it was a shared Soviet tragedy, not a crime against Ukrainians.

Add it all up, the Red Terror, the famines, the camps and the purges, and Soviet communism killed millions upon millions of its own people; by many historians’ counts, more than the six million Jews murdered in the Holocaust. That is not a contest, and it should not be one. The Holocaust is remembered because survivors, historians and whole nations worked to make sure it would be. The Holodomor was buried on purpose, by the people who caused it and the reporters who covered for them.

Today Russian missiles are falling on the same Ukrainian towns that starved in 1933, and the Kremlin still talks about Ukraine as a country that does not really exist. Forgetting a genocide does not make it end. It makes it easier to do again.
